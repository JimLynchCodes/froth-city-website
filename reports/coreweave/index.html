CoreWeave: Debt, Rates, and the Data-Center Crossfire

CoreWeave's scheduled debt principal was $35.6 billion at June 30, 2026, and quarterly interest expense already exceeds operating income. The book is concentrated in AI infrastructure demand, including a large commitment from OpenAI, while local fights over power, water, and zoning are slowing the data-center buildout the debt is funding.

The position

CoreWeave rents GPU capacity, mostly to companies training and running AI models. The equity pitch is the contracted backlog. The credit question is the balance sheet underneath it. We are short CRWV because the build is being funded with a large, mostly high-coupon debt stack, because interest expense already overwhelms operating income, and because the revenue that has to service that debt is concentrated in AI infrastructure spending. If that spending slows, both the cash that pays the coupons and the market that refinances the principal can shrink at the same time. That is an opinion about risk. It is not a prediction that a particular maturity will be missed.

What the quarter shows

For the quarter ended June 30, 2026, CoreWeave reported revenue of $2,575 million and an operating loss of $49 million. Interest expense, net was $640 million, compared with $267 million in the year-earlier quarter. Management attributed the increase of $373 million, or 140%, primarily to higher borrowing and a larger debt balance. Net loss was $626 million. For the six months, revenue was $4,653 million, the operating loss was $193 million, interest expense, net was $1,176 million against $531 million a year earlier, and the net loss was $1,366 million.

The same filing shows how much cash the build is already absorbing. Purchases of property and equipment, including capitalized internal-use software, were $14,117 million in the first half of 2026, against $3,860 million in the 2025 period. Net cash used in investing was $14,874 million. Net cash provided by operating activities was $3,663 million. That operating figure is after interest paid, net of amounts capitalized, of $806 million. Cash dedicated to debt service in the half was about $6.2 billion: $5.2 billion of principal and $982 million of interest, including $176 million of capitalized interest.

The debt, and what is not the debt

At June 30, 2026, recourse debt, net of unamortized discount and issuance costs, was $31,405 million. Of that, $6,235 million was current and $25,170 million was non-current. Non-recourse debt, net, was $3,663 million, limited to certain subsidiaries except for specified bad-act guarantees. Future principal payments on total debt were $4,413 million for the rest of 2026, $6,184 million in 2027, $4,416 million in 2028, $2,421 million in 2029, $3,221 million in 2030, and $14,896 million thereafter. The total is $35,551 million.

Those principal amounts are the schedule in the debt footnote. They are not facility sizes. In March 2026 CoreWeave put in place a DDTL 4.0 facility of $8,500 million, priced at SOFR plus 2.25% or Treasury plus 2.00%. In May 2026 it put in place a DDTL 5.0 facility of $3,100 million at SOFR plus 4.50%. Drawn balances were smaller. At June 30, DDTL 5.0 had $1,101 million outstanding, and the non-recourse DDTL 4.0 facility had $2,837 million outstanding. Management separately reported $13.6 billion outstanding under delayed-draw term loans and $16.6 billion of aggregate outstanding principal on notes.

Effective rates on the drawn book run from 2% on the convertible notes to 15% on the oldest delayed-draw loan. Senior-note effective rates cluster around 9% to 10%. The weighted-average rate on short-term OEM financing was 9%. Operating lease liabilities were a further $16.3 billion. Those leases are not inside the $35.6 billion principal schedule. The accumulated deficit was $4,009 million.

Remaining performance obligations — contracted revenue not yet recognized, net of estimated variable consideration — were $103.7 billion. CoreWeave expected to recognize 41% of that by June 30, 2028, 39% in the following two years, and the rest between months 49 and 78. RPO is a backlog figure. It is not cash on hand, and it is not the capital expenditure required to deliver the capacity.

Rates and the wall in front of the company

A large part of the stack is floating-rate term loans priced over SOFR, or in one case Treasury, sitting next to senior notes with coupons from 9.00% to 9.75%. The convertibles, at a 2% effective rate, are the cheap slice. They are not most of the book. Interest expense, net of $640 million in a single quarter is already an order of magnitude larger than the quarter's $49 million operating loss. A company that merely broke even on operations would still be handing the entire operating result, and more, to lenders.

CoreWeave quantified the floating-rate exposure. For every 100 basis point move in interest rates, interest expense could rise or fall by about $30 million in a quarter and $61 million over six months, based on the floating-rate balance at June 30, 2026. The nearer problem is principal. About $4.4 billion comes due in the remainder of 2026 and $6.2 billion in 2027. Refinancing that paper, or drawing more on facilities priced at a spread over SOFR, costs more if policy rates stay elevated, and it requires credit markets that are still willing to fund AI infrastructure.

Equity has been a supplement, not a replacement for the amortization schedule. The March 2025 IPO of 37 million Class A shares at $40 produced $1.4 billion of net proceeds, before $31 million of offering costs, and the underwriters later bought about 2 million more shares for $68 million of net proceeds. In January 2026, NVIDIA bought about 23 million shares at $87.20, for $2.0 billion gross. In April 2026 the company sold about 9 million shares at $109, for $1.0 billion gross.

Who the revenue depends on

In the second quarter of 2026, three customers accounted for 36%, 26%, and 10% of revenue. For the six months, the top two were 40% and 23%. Customers A and B each accounted for 32% of accounts receivable at June 30, 2026. In the year-earlier quarter the largest customer was 71% of revenue. The filing says the letter labels are not necessarily the same parties from one period to the next.

The 2025 Form 10-K names one of them. CoreWeave recognized approximately 67% of revenue from Microsoft for the year ended December 31, 2025, and no other customer was 10% or more that year. Microsoft is a profitable public company. So is Meta Platforms. In March 2026 Meta signed an order form that CoreWeave describes as an initial commitment of up to about $21.0 billion, covering new capacity through December 20, 2032 and the exercise of an existing option through April 10, 2032. In April 2026 Jane Street, which the filing identifies as a private company, committed about $6.0 billion. The filing lists Microsoft and OpenAI together as other significant customers.

The customer that matches the “unprofitable AI lab” description is OpenAI, not Microsoft or Meta. Under a May 2025 master services agreement and a September 2025 order form, OpenAI OpCo, LLC committed to pay CoreWeave up to about $6.5 billion through May 31, 2031. The same risk-factor discussion says the company expects to add early-stage and private customers, and that those customers can carry a higher risk of non-payment. We are not folding Microsoft, Meta, or Jane Street into that description.

OpenAI does not file these numbers with the SEC. Audited statements obtained by Ed Zitron and reviewed by the Financial Times, as reported by Quartz and Ars Technica in June 2026, show 2025 revenue of $13.07 billion and an operating loss of $20.92 billion. The headline net loss was about $38.5 billion and included a large charge tied to the conversion from a nonprofit structure. We have not inspected the statements ourselves. We use the operating loss, which those accounts treat as the cleaner figure, and we treat it as leaked reporting rather than a number from a CoreWeave filing.

The short is therefore not “every customer loses money.” It is that revenue is concentrated in AI infrastructure demand, that one named forward commitment is to a private company whose leaked 2025 accounts show a very large operating loss, and that the 2025 book was dominated by a single hyperscaler whose AI budget is a capital-allocation choice.

The towns in the way

The capacity behind both the debt and the backlog has to be built in particular places, and several of those places are pushing back. None of the figures in this section come from the 10-Q. They are from local reporting, and we are not treating any project as cancelled.

In Kenilworth, New Jersey, NJ.com reported a roughly $1.8 billion, nearly 400,000-square-foot AI data center on a former pharmaceutical headquarters. Construction had begun in September, with operations expected in early 2027. A petition started by a nearby resident had drawn more than 4,000 signatures when that story ran. Union News Daily reported protests ahead of a May 19, 2026 planning board meeting. Cranford, next door, has moved to take data centers out of the uses permitted in its commercial zones. The township mayor said Cranford had no advance notice of the Kenilworth project. We are not relying on petition claims about megawatts or tax credits that we have not seen in a news report.

In Lancaster, Pennsylvania, LancasterOnline has reported CoreWeave data centers planned at 216 Greenfield Road and 1375 Harrisburg Pike. The city's zoning officer treated them as “wholesale trade and storage,” a classification residents wanted reviewed in a public hearing. The developers went to court and blocked that appeal. Work on the first Greenfield Road building was underway, with an opening expected in summer 2027, and the developers told the court they had already spent $1.3 billion on it. A later city ordinance regulating data centers was written so that applications already on file would keep the prior rules.

Power, water, and zoning fights do not have to stop a project to matter. They can push the date a site begins to earn. Interest on the debt raised to build it does not wait for the substation.

What would have to go right

CoreWeave can point to a $103.7 billion contracted backlog and to customers that include Microsoft and Meta. It can also point to a $35.6 billion principal schedule, a quarter in which interest expense was $640 million against a $49 million operating loss, and more than $10 billion of principal due before the end of 2027. If AI infrastructure spending stays strong and the sites get power, the backlog can carry a great deal of debt. If it does not, a company with this much leverage and this much customer concentration can be badly hurt. In a sharp unwind, we think the damage could be fatal. That is the short.
