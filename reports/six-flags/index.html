FUN ▼ Short Leisure / Theme Parks Oct 12, 2026

Six Flags: The Business of Fun Isn't Much Fun

Five reasons, in the order we would underwrite them. The June quarter did not earn its interest. The equity account is $115 million under $5.0 billion of debt. The parks they kept are not a visit collapse. The reported attendance drop is mostly parks they sold.

Executive summary

We are short Six Flags Entertainment Corporation, ticker FUN on the New York Stock Exchange. The company is the combination of the former Six Flags and Cedar Fair. The merger closed on July 1, 2024. Headquarters in the filing is 8701 Red Oak Blvd., Charlotte, North Carolina. This note uses only information that was public on October 12, 2026. The last financial statements are the Form 10-Q for the quarter ended June 28, 2026, filed August 6, 2026. The third-quarter report was filed on November 5, 2026. It is not in this note.1

The business is seasonal, leveraged, and still digesting a merger. In the June quarter, the one that should be good, operating income was $88.6 million and net interest expense was $102.1 million. For the six months, adjusted EBITDA was $120.0 million and net interest expense was $197.0 million. Gross debt principal was $5.017 billion. Book equity was $114.7 million. Cash was $134.5 million. Goodwill was $2.078 billion.1

Reported attendance fell. Same-park attendance did not. On the parks still open, visits rose 3.5% in the quarter and 3.6% in the half, and same-park net revenue rose 4.3% in the half. The short is not "nobody came." The short is that the estate does not earn the coupon, the equity is an accounting remainder after a $1.52 billion impairment in 2025, and the cash that reduced the revolver came from selling seven parks below book.1

01 The coupon is larger than the good quarter

The second quarter is inside the season. It is not the whole season. July and August, which fall in the third quarter, can be nearly half of full-year attendance and more than half of full-year earnings, on the company's own description of 2025. Even so, the June quarter is when the parks are open and the income statement should look like a business that can carry debt. Operating income was $88.6 million, up from $74.5 million. Net interest expense was $102.1 million, up from $92.4 million. Operating income covered 87 cents of each dollar of interest. Net loss attributable to Six Flags was $202.6 million, against $99.6 million a year earlier. A large piece of that gap is tax: the provision was $157.4 million, against $76.3 million. The operating comparison does not need the tax line. Interest alone is larger than operating income.1

The six-month picture is plainer. Operating loss was $223.6 million, against $246.5 million. Adjusted EBITDA was $120.0 million, against $71.8 million. Net interest expense was $197.0 million, against $179.4 million. Adjusted EBITDA covered 61 cents of each dollar of interest for the half. The company says interest rose largely because the 2027 senior notes and the 2027 Six Notes were refinanced with 8.625% senior notes due 2032. There was also a $4.1 million loss on early extinguishment. The refinancing removed a 2027 wall. It replaced it with a higher coupon. Likelihood is high because both numbers are in the statements. Impact is severe because this is the quarter that is supposed to carry the year, and it does not cover the coupon on a GAAP operating basis. Timing is the quarter already filed, not a hypothetical 2031 maturity.1

Quarterly adjusted EBITDA was $243.1 million, against $242.6 million. On that non-GAAP measure the June quarter does cover interest, about 2.4 times. We do not ignore that. We also do not let it outrank the GAAP operating result or the half-year, where the add-backs include merger costs, severance, the disposal loss, and the trade-name impairment. Those add-backs are defined in the credit agreement. They are still cash or still a reduction in what the parks are worth.1

02 The equity is what is left

At June 28, 2026, gross principal was $5.017 billion. The long-term carrying amount, after issuance costs and fair-value layers, was $4.972 billion, plus a $15.0 million current portion of the term loan. Cash was $134.5 million. Total equity was $114.7 million. Goodwill was $2.078 billion. The common stockholders' account is smaller than the goodwill and smaller than one quarter of interest.1

Equity did not start the year at $115 million. It was $549.8 million at December 31, 2025, and $2.042 billion at December 31, 2024. The 2025 collapse includes a cumulative $1.52 billion impairment in the third quarter of 2025, when management concluded that estimated fair values no longer covered carrying values at Six Flags Fiesta Texas, Great Adventure, Great America, Magic Mountain, Mexico, Over Georgia, Over Texas, and Schlitterbahn, and at certain trade names. The company says that quarter can be nearly half of annual attendance and more than half of earnings, so the miss was not a shoulder-season miss. In the first half of 2026 the company recorded another $38.6 million impairment of the Six Flags and Schlitterbahn trade names because the parks in the sale no longer contributed revenue to those names. The filing also says future valuation assumptions could change and produce further impairment. Likelihood that the equity account is thin is high, because it is the balance sheet. Impact is severe relative to $5.0 billion of principal. Timing is now. A 2031 maturity is not required for this reason.12

03 Capex, weather days, and park sales

In 2025, cash from operations was $327.5 million and payments for productive assets were $479.7 million. The parks did not fund their own capital spending. In the first half of 2026, cash from operations was $152.7 million and capital expenditures were $149.5 million. That is roughly a wash before anything else. Investing cash flow was a source of $108.4 million only because proceeds from the sale of parks were $257.9 million. The sale is not a recurring operating result.12

The 2026 sale covered seven parks: Worlds of Fun, Michigan's Adventure, Valleyfair, Six Flags Great Escape, Schlitterbahn Waterpark Galveston, Six Flags St. Louis, and Six Flags La Ronde. U.S. closings were April 6, 2026. The Canadian closing was May 15, 2026. The company recorded a $37.8 million loss because the adjusted price was below the net book value of the disposal group. Management describes the sale as portfolio optimization that should narrow focus, cut ongoing capital spending, and limit liabilities. The accounting result in the half is a loss, plus the trade-name impairment that followed from removing those revenues. Partnership parks also have a minimum capital-expenditure covenant, generally 6% of those parks' revenues over rolling five-year periods. The company says 2026 spending is expected to exceed that minimum. Capex does not go to zero because seven parks were sold.1

Weather is already in the same-park day count. Same-park operating days fell by 68 in the half, which the company attributes to dropping low-volume days and to unfavorable weather at a few water parks. Attendance is defined as guest visits, and the filing lists weather among the drivers, with new rides, satisfaction, pricing, advertising, perceived safety, and economic conditions. We do not have a dollar figure that isolates weather from the decision to close cheap days. We do have the company's statement that the cost base is largely fixed for a season and does not move much with attendance. A lost weekend is not matched by a lost week of wages. Likelihood that weather and the calendar matter is high. Impact is high in a fixed-cost park. Timing is every summer, including the one that had already ended when this note was written and had not yet been reported.1

04 The merger is not finished

The July 1, 2024 merger is still an add-back. Costs related to the mergers were $3.7 million in the quarter and $8.6 million in the half, against $11.0 million and $26.7 million a year earlier. The footnote describes third-party consulting, information-technology integration, integration-team pay, retention bonuses, maintenance to bring former Six Flags parks to Cedar Fair standards, and certain legal costs. Severance was $16.7 million in the quarter and $17.0 million in the half. Both are added back to reach adjusted EBITDA. The spending is smaller than last year. It is not zero.1

Reported results are also not comparable to last year, and the company says so. Six-month operating days fell by 402, of which 334 were the parks that are no longer operating, including the Bowie, Maryland park that closed after the 2025 season and the seven parks in the sale. Reported attendance was 13.128 million in the quarter, down 7.5% from 14.191 million, and 16.051 million in the half, down 5.6% from 17.009 million. Same-park attendance was 13.128 million against 12.679 million in the quarter, up 3.5%, and 16.051 million against 15.497 million in the half, up 3.6%. The visit increase was season-pass and membership visitation, a larger active pass base, and more cross-park visits. Same-park net revenue was $864.5 million in the quarter, up 2.4%, and $1,085.3 million in the half, up 4.3%. Per capita spending on a same-park basis rose $0.31, or 0.5%, in the half.1

A short that stops at "attendance is down 7.5%" is using the parks they sold as if they were a demand shock. We rank the merger and the sale fourth because the integration costs and the below-book sale are real, and because the reported decline will keep confusing the tape. We do not rank a visit collapse. The parks they kept had more guests. Likelihood is high. Impact is high because the sale is how the revolver was paid down, not because the guest left. Timing is the quarter in which the sales closed.1

05 The year is a summer

About 70% of 2025 attendance and net revenue fell in the second and third quarters. The company expects a substantial portion of revenue from Memorial Day through Labor Day, with the largest piece in July and August. The fall Halloween season also matters. The third quarter of 2025 is the period in which the $1.52 billion impairment was taken, after management said it finally had a clear view of the peak. As of October 12, 2026, that same peak for 2026 has happened on the calendar and has not happened in a filing. We will not invent the July and August numbers. We will say the last public quarter is not the quarter that decides the year, and the last time that quarter disappointed, the company wrote off $1.52 billion.12

Consumer demand, as a separate claim, is weaker than the debt claim. The filing lists economic conditions as one driver of attendance. It does not say the guest stopped spending. Same-park per capita was up. Reported admissions per capita in the quarter was $33.62, down $0.57 from $34.19, while in-park product per capita was $29.27, up $1.00. Out-of-park revenue on a reported basis fell $7.6 million in the quarter. We do not promote a consumer recession we cannot quote. The consumer shows up here as a risk to a fixed-cost summer, not as reason number one.1

07 Risks to the short

Same-park results are the first risk. Visits were up. Revenue was up. Half-year adjusted EBITDA rose from $71.8 million to $120.0 million. Quarterly adjusted EBITDA was flat and did cover interest. If the third quarter, once it is filed, looks like that half rather than like the 2025 peak, reason 1 gets weaker on the measure creditors use. We would still have a GAAP operating result in the June quarter that did not cover interest, but the direction of the parks they kept would be against the short.1

Liquidity is not the near-term story. The revolver had $90 million drawn. Letters of credit were $57.2 million. Availability was $702.8 million at June 28. The 2027 notes were refinanced into 2032. A short that needs a 2026 default is the wrong short. The term loan amortizes $15 million a year and matures in 2031. The coupon is the problem. A maturity wall this winter is not.1

Selling parks can be the right capital decision even at a book loss if the sold parks would have consumed cash. Management says the sales should reduce capital spending and liabilities. If the remaining estate earns the 8.625% coupon on the new $1 billion of notes and the rest of the stack, the equity at $115 million of book can rerate without any help from a higher visit count. That is the risk. It is not what the June quarter, taken alone, already did.

08 Other considerations

These are real, and they are not the numbered short.

The attendance headline. Reported visits fell because parks closed and were sold. Same-park visits rose. Anyone who leads with the 7.5% decline without the same-park table is describing a portfolio change as a demand change.1

The operating chief. On July 1, 2026, the company said Tim Fisher would leave the chief operating officer role effective July 15, 2026, and remain as special advisor to the chief executive through December 15, 2026, under an amendment dated June 25. On July 2 it appointed Mark Pauls, age 63, formerly senior vice president of operations at Herschend Family Entertainment. A leadership change in July can matter in a summer business. It is not why interest exceeded operating income.3

The merger suit. A complaint alleges that the registration statement and prospectus for the July 1, 2024 merger failed to disclose that former Six Flags had underinvested in its parks and that the financial plans were not reasonably achievable. Competing lead-plaintiff motions have been pending since January 2026. Defendants had not yet responded and said they intend to defend. A derivative complaint was filed November 25, 2025. We do not have a damages number. The underinvestment claim, if it is true, is part of why the merger integration and the 2025 impairment exist. It is not a separate cash item in the June quarter.1

Tax and non-controlling interests. The quarter's tax provision of $157.4 million is larger than operating income. The company ties the increase to the estimated annual rate, the non-controlling interest distribution, accretion on the Six Flags Over Georgia call option, and non-deductible compensation. Net income attributable to non-controlling interests was $25.1 million in both the quarter and the half. Adjusted EBITDA subtracts that amount. The Georgia and Texas partnership structure is a claim on cash that common stockholders do not fully own. We leave it here because the coupon comparison already stands without it.1
